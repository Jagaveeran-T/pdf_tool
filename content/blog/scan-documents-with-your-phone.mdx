You do not need a scanner to turn paper into a PDF. A phone camera, decent light and a few minutes of care can produce results that are perfectly readable for forms, receipts, signed pages and homework. The difference between a good phone scan and a frustrating one is mostly technique, not equipment.

This article walks through the whole process: preparing the page, taking the photo, saving it in a suitable format, keeping multiple pages in order, converting to PDF, controlling file size, and handling scans of private documents sensibly.

## Before you take the photo

### Choose your light

Light is the single biggest factor. Aim for bright, even, diffuse light.

- Daylight near a window works well, but avoid direct sun hitting the paper, which creates glare and harsh contrast.
- Indoors, turn on the main lights and stand so that your own body does not cast a shadow across the page.
- Avoid the phone flash. It often produces a bright hotspot, especially on glossy paper, and uneven brightness across the sheet.
- If the paper is glossy, tilt it slightly or shift your position until reflections disappear from the screen preview.

A quick test is to look at the preview: if one corner is noticeably darker, adjust before you shoot.

### Use a flat, contrasting surface

Place the paper on a flat desk or table. A surface that contrasts with the paper helps, such as a dark desk for white paper, because it makes the edges obvious. A wrinkled page casts tiny shadows and bends text lines, so smooth it with your hand, or place a clear sheet of glass or a clean book over it if you can do so without covering the text.

For stapled or bound documents, flatten the pages as best you can and keep your fingers out of the frame, or at the very edges where they hide nothing important.

### Clear the lens

Phone lenses pick up fingerprints. A quick wipe with a soft clean cloth makes pictures noticeably sharper. It sounds trivial, but a smeared lens produces a soft, hazy scan that no later step can fully fix.

## Taking the photo

### Frame the page

- Hold the phone parallel to the paper, directly above it, rather than at an angle. Tilting produces a trapezoid, where the near edge looks wider than the far edge, and text near the far edge gets smaller.
- Fill most of the frame with the page, leaving a small border so no corner gets cut off.
- Keep the phone steady. Rest your elbows on the table or use both hands. Some people prop a phone against a stack of books.
- Let the camera focus. Tap the text on screen if your phone supports tap-to-focus, and wait a moment before pressing the shutter.
- Take the picture in the orientation that matches the page, so a portrait page is held in portrait. The orientation tag stored with the photo is described in [page ordering and rotation tips](/blog/page-ordering-and-rotation-tips/).

If your phone has a grid overlay in its camera settings, switch it on. It makes it easier to see whether the page edges line up with the lines.

### Check each shot immediately

Open the photo and zoom in on the smallest text. Can you read it comfortably? Are all four corners visible? Is any part washed out by glare? Retaking now costs seconds. Discovering a blurry page after you have returned the original to its owner costs far more.

## Resolution: enough, but not excessive

Modern phone photos often have 12 million pixels or more, for example 4000 by 3000. That is plenty. For an A4 page (210 by 297 mm, about 8.27 by 11.69 inches), 300 pixels per inch would need around 2480 by 3510 pixels, so even a modest phone camera meets that when the page fills the frame.

If your camera app offers a choice of resolution or quality, a medium or high setting is fine. Maximum is rarely necessary for ordinary text. You can also reduce size later: the article on [keeping PDF file size down](/blog/reduce-pdf-file-size-tips/) explains how to resize photos before conversion.

## Built-in scanner features in general

Many phones, and some note-taking and cloud-storage apps, include a document-scanning mode. Without getting into specific products, these features generally try to help with some or all of the following:

- Detect the edges of the paper automatically.
- Correct perspective so a slightly tilted shot looks square.
- Boost contrast so the background looks whiter and the text darker.
- Remove shadows or apply a black-and-white or greyscale filter.
- Capture several pages in a row and let you reorder them.

Features differ between devices and software versions, so look in your camera app or the app you use for notes and files to see what is available. Using such a mode is optional. A plain camera photo works too, and you may prefer it because it gives you the raw picture with no filter applied.

Two cautions. First, filters that boost contrast can wash out faint pencil, stamps or light-coloured signatures, so check the result. Second, some scanner features save directly to PDF in their own app. That can be convenient, but if you want to combine pages from several sources or control the page order and size yourself, saving individual JPEG images and converting them separately gives you more control.

## Saving as JPG

For the converter on this site, you want JPEG files. These are typically named with the extension .jpg or .jpeg. Many phones can save in other formats by default, such as HEIC or HEIF, which some devices use to save space. [JPG to PDF](/jpg-to-pdf/) accepts only JPEG files, and it checks the file signature rather than trusting the extension, so renaming a HEIC file to .jpg will not make it work.

Options if your phone saves in another format:

- Look in the camera settings for a "most compatible" or "JPEG" format option.
- Export or share the photo as a JPEG from your gallery app. Many apps convert automatically when you share or export.
- If you use a scanner mode, check whether it lets you export pages as images.

[Image to PDF](/image-to-pdf/) additionally accepts PNG and WebP, which helps if you have screenshots or images saved from messaging apps. See [JPG vs PNG for PDF](/blog/jpg-vs-png-for-pdf/) for when each format suits best.

## Multi-page documents and order

For a document with several pages, order is easy to lose track of.

1. Photograph the pages in sequence, one after another, without skipping around.
2. Include every page, including blank back sides if they matter, for example if the back carries a signature or stamp.
3. After shooting, scroll through the gallery and confirm that all the pages are there and in order.
4. Rename or move the photos into one folder with numbered names such as lease-01.jpg, lease-02.jpg, if you want predictable sorting.
5. Add them to the converter and check the list. You can drag to reorder, and remove any retakes or duplicates.

Phone galleries sort by date taken, so if you retake page 4 after page 6, the retake appears out of sequence. That is the most common reason a scan comes out wrongly ordered. Fix it in the converter by dragging the retake into position, and read the thumbnails afterwards to be sure.

## Converting with JPG to PDF

Here is the short sequence:

1. Open [JPG to PDF](/jpg-to-pdf/) in your browser.
2. Add your photos. The tool accepts up to 50 photos, each up to 50 MB, with a 200 MB total.
3. Drag them into the right order.
4. Choose a page size. The default, "Fit to image", gives each page the shape of its photo. A4 or US Letter places each photo on a standard sheet, centred and scaled to fit, never cropped. If you plan to print or submit the file to an office that expects standard pages, A4 or Letter is usually the more sensible choice.
5. Choose a margin: none, small or medium.
6. Create the PDF and download it.

The tool embeds your original JPEG data without re-compressing it, with one exception: a photo that carries an EXIF rotation flag is redrawn upright and saved again as JPEG at quality 0.92, so it is very slightly lossy. Photos that arrive sideways with no flag can be turned with the Rotate control on each photo (90-degree steps), or fixed in a photo viewer first. The site cannot rotate pages inside a finished PDF.

You will find a fuller walkthrough in [how to convert images to PDF](/blog/how-to-convert-images-to-pdf/).

## File size

Because the original JPEG data is kept, the PDF is roughly as large as your photos combined. Ten 4 MB photos produce a PDF of around 40 MB. That may be too large for an email attachment. Before converting, consider:

- Resizing photos to something like 1600 to 2500 pixels on the long side.
- Leaving out pages you do not need.
- Splitting the finished PDF into parts with [Split PDF](/split-pdf/), if it must go by email.

The [Compress PDF](/compress-pdf/) tool can shrink a finished PDF, but it is lossy and cannot promise a size, so planning the size at the photo stage is still the better route.

## Privacy of your scans

Scans of documents tend to be the most personal files on a phone: identity papers, bank letters, medical forms, contracts. A few habits help.

- The conversion tools here run in your browser, and files are not uploaded by them. See [is it safe to use online PDF tools](/blog/is-it-safe-to-use-online-pdf-tools/) for how to verify that yourself and what the limits of that statement are.
- Remember that the originals are still in your photo gallery, which may back up automatically to a cloud account. Delete scans you no longer need, and empty any recently-deleted folder if your gallery has one.
- Do not send sensitive scans through channels you would not use for the paper original.
- If you only need to share some pages, extract just those.
- Cover or crop out information that the recipient does not need, if that is acceptable for the purpose, before you build the PDF.
- Check requirements: some institutions insist on original documents or specific formats for official submissions.
